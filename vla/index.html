Vision-Language-Action models, end to end

How I think a robot foundation model gets made: the π0 / OpenVLA / GR00T-style recipe, from internet pretraining to a bimanual robot running the policy on the table. Each of the eight stages has a live 3D scene next to it.

The pipeline

8 stages, one model

A vision-language-action model (VLA) is a robot policy built on a vision-language model. It looks at camera images, reads an instruction like “put the cube in the bin”, knows where its own joints are, and outputs motor commands. The idea that made these work is that you don't start from zero. You take a model that has already learned from the internet what objects, words and scenes are, attach an action output to it, and teach it to move with robot demonstrations.

RT-2 named the category in 2023. Since then OpenVLA, Octo, π0 and π0.5, GR00T N1, Helix, Gemini Robotics and SmolVLA have settled into roughly the same recipe, with differences in the details. This post is how I think about that recipe, as the eight stages in the graph below, from internet pretraining to a policy running in a control loop on the robot.

πθ( at … at+H−1 | It1 … Itn, ℓ, qt ) a: action chunk · I: camera images · ℓ: instruction · q: joint state

In words: given the latest images, the instruction and the joint state, predict the next H actions (an action chunk). Everything below is about getting that one function right, then making it run fast enough on the robot.

01

VLM pretraining

Internet image + texttraining

Everything starts with a vision-language model (VLM) trained on web-scale image–text data. A vision encoder cuts each image into patches and turns them into tokens. A language model then reads those tokens alongside the text and learns to predict what comes next. π0 starts from PaliGemma (3B). OpenVLA uses the Prismatic VLM, which puts Llama 2 7B on top of fused DINOv2 and SigLIP features. GR00T N1 uses NVIDIA's Eagle-2, and Gemini Robotics builds on Gemini 2.0.

Why bother, when the goal is a robot? Because robot data is tiny and expensive, while the web is huge. A web-trained VLM already knows what a mug is, which drawer is “the top one”, and that a banana goes in a bowl. RT-2 showed that this transfers: co-trained with web data, its robot could follow instructions about objects and ideas that never appeared in its robot data.

The catch is that most VLMs were trained for captions and question answering on low-resolution images. They are weak at precise spatial work such as pointing, depth and small objects, which is exactly what manipulation needs. That's why newer recipes add embodied-reasoning data (points, boxes, trajectories, subtask labels) before or during robot training. Gemini Robotics-ER and π0.5 are good examples.

image patches + text tokens
objective
next-token prediction (plus contrastive pretraining for the encoder)

you get
a backbone that understands scenes and language
02

Robot data

Teleop demosdata

This is the expensive part, and I think it's the part that decides who wins. The standard method is teleoperation: a person drives the robot, and every timestep is logged with the camera images, the joint state and the commanded action, plus a language label for the episode.

The scene shows an ALOHA-style leader–follower rig. The operator moves two small leader arms, and the follower arms copy their joint angles at 50 Hz. It's cheap, it feels natural for two-handed work, and the actions come out in exactly the robot's own joint space. Other options are VR teleop (common for humanoids), space mice, GELLO-style puppets, handheld grippers like UMI that record demos without a robot, and plain human video.

A dataset is a pile of these episodes. Open X-Embodiment pooled more than 1M trajectories from 22 robot types. DROID collected 76k Franka trajectories (about 350 hours) across 564 scenes. π0's own mixture had over 10,000 hours from 7 robot configurations.

Quality matters more than count. You want consistent operators, demos without hesitation, wide variety in scenes and starting positions, and some recovery behaviour (start from a mistake and show the fix). Mixed-quality data is fine for pretraining but hurts during post-training.

03

VLA pretraining

Add action headtraining

Now the VLM learns to act. There are two main ways to get actions out of it.

  • Discrete tokens. Split each action dimension into 256 bins and have the language model predict them as tokens (RT-2, OpenVLA). It's simple and reuses the language head. But it needs one forward pass per token, which is slow, and the bins are coarse for fast, dexterous control. FAST (π0-FAST) fixes much of this by compressing whole action chunks with a DCT and BPE before tokenizing.
  • A continuous action head. A separate action expert outputs continuous actions with diffusion or flow matching (Octo, π0, GR00T N1, SmolVLA). In π0, a ~300M-parameter expert sits next to PaliGemma, attends to its tokens layer by layer, and turns noise into a 50-step action chunk in about 10 integration steps. That's what the 3D view shows.

Action chunking itself (predicting H steps at once, from ACT) is the other key idea. It smooths motion, copes with demos that solve a task in different ways, and lets a slow model drive a fast control loop.

Pretraining mixes everything: single arms, bimanual rigs, mobile manipulators and humanoids, each with a different action space, padded to a common size. Some web data stays in the mix so the VLM doesn't forget what it knew (co-training). π0.5 also predicts the next subtask in text before acting, and “knowledge insulation” stops the action expert's gradients from degrading the VLM.

in
images + instruction + joint state (+ noisy actions)
out
a chunk of H = 50 actions (1 s at 50 Hz)
loss
flow matching or diffusion (expert); cross-entropy (tokens)
size
~3B backbone + ~300M expert (π0)
04

Post-training

Target robot + tasktraining

The pretrained VLA is a generalist that does many things reasonably well. Post-training specializes it to one robot and one family of tasks with a smaller, cleaner dataset. That might be tens to hundreds of hours for hard, dexterous tasks like folding laundry, or a few hundred demos for simple pick-and-place.

Full fine-tuning gives the best results if you have the compute. LoRA on the backbone (OpenVLA), or training only the action expert with the VLM frozen (GR00T N1.5), gets most of the way there on a single GPU. OpenVLA-OFT showed that the fine-tuning recipe matters as much as the base model: parallel decoding, action chunks and continuous actions with an L1 loss made it much faster and more accurate.

The things that tend to move the success rate:

  • Match the action space and control rate to the robot (joint vs. end-effector, absolute vs. delta).
  • Normalize each action dimension with quantiles, not min/max. One outlier can ruin min/max.
  • Curate: drop failed or hesitant demos, and balance the task variants.
  • Hold out whole scenes or objects for evaluation, not random frames.
data
50 to 1,000+ demos per task family
method
full fine-tune, LoRA, or frozen VLM + trained expert
watch
overfitting to one scene, forgetting language
05

RL / refinement

Optional, risingtraining

Imitation learning tops out around the quality of the demonstrations, and it never learns from its own mistakes. Small errors compound into states the demos never covered. RL closes that gap. The graph marks it “optional, rising” because 2025 is when it started working for VLAs at scale.

  • In simulation: many parallel environments and a sparse success reward. SimpleVLA-RL applies GRPO-style RL with a binary success reward to an OpenVLA-OFT policy on simulation benchmarks. That's the 3D view: nine environments, failures flash, and the success rate climbs each iteration.
  • On real robots: learn from the robot's own attempts plus human corrections. π*0.6's Recap trains a value function on that experience and conditions the policy on the advantage. On some of the hardest tasks (espresso, laundry, box assembly) it more than doubled throughput and roughly halved the failure rate.
  • Steer instead of fine-tune: DSRL runs RL in the latent noise space of a frozen diffusion or flow policy, so it changes behaviour without touching the weights.

The hard parts are reward design for real tasks, resets, and safety while exploring. That's why most real-world RL today starts from a strong imitation policy and keeps a human in the loop.

06

Evaluation

Sim + real trialsdeploy / eval

Evaluating a robot policy is harder than training one. Offline metrics such as action error on held-out demos barely correlate with real success, so you need rollouts.

  • Simulation benchmarks: LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv (built to track real Google Robot and WidowX results) and RoboCasa. They're cheap and reproducible, but many are near saturation, and the sim-to-real gap never fully goes away.
  • Real trials: a fixed task suite with randomized starting positions, run A/B against a baseline and scored by success (often with partial credit). RoboArena spreads evaluation across labs with pairwise comparisons.

The statistics matter more than people think. 16 successes in 20 trials is “80%”, but the 95% Wilson interval is 58–92%. Two policies 10 points apart on 20 trials are usually indistinguishable. The tally boards in the 3D view show this interval live.

Every failure gets triaged (perception, grasp, recovery, language grounding) and fed back as targeted data or an RL signal. That's the dashed loop in the graph. Evaluation also gates the optimized model: if a quantized build regresses, it goes back.

07

Optimize for edge

Compress + compiledeploy / eval

A 3B-parameter model in bf16 is about 6.6 GB of weights. It has to run on the robot, because cloud inference adds network jitter a control loop can't absorb. It also has to be fast enough to keep the action queue full on something like a Jetson AGX Orin (up to 275 sparse INT8 TOPS) or the newer Jetson Thor.

  • Quantize: bf16 to FP8 or INT8, and INT4 (AWQ, GPTQ) for the language-model weights. Check layer by layer; the action head is usually more sensitive than the backbone.
  • Do less work: fewer flow steps (10 to 5), fewer vision tokens (lower resolution, token pruning), and cache the image + text prefix so only the action expert reruns during denoising.
  • Compile: export to TensorRT (or use torch.compile with CUDA graphs), use fused attention kernels, and fuse small ops such as the Q, K and V projections.
  • Split the model: run a slow “System 2” VLM at roughly 7–10 Hz and a fast “System 1” policy at 120–200 Hz (GR00T N1, Helix).

Then re-evaluate. Compression that looks free on benchmarks can quietly cost a few points of success on the real robot.

08

Deployment

Robot control loopdeploy / eval

On the robot, the policy runs a receding-horizon loop: read the images and joint state, run the VLA, get a chunk of 50 actions, and execute them at 50 Hz while the next chunk is computed. In the 3D view, the dotted path ahead of each gripper is the current chunk. It refreshes every 0.5 s, so the robot only executes the first half before replanning.

  • Latency vs. chunk boundaries. If inference takes 100 ms and the robot waits for it, the arm stalls. If it switches chunks blindly, the arm jerks. Temporal ensembling (ACT) blends overlapping chunks. Real-time chunking (RTC) inpaints the next chunk so it agrees with the actions already committed.
  • Safety lives below the policy. Joint limits, velocity and torque clamps, workspace bounds and an e-stop sit in the controller, never inside the model.
  • Log everything. Deployment failures are the best training data you'll ever get, so the loop closes back to stage 02.
loop
observe → infer → act, then repeat
rates
a new chunk every 0.5 s · joint commands at 50 Hz
chunk
H = 50 actions (1 s); execute 25, then replan

What I think is still open

  • Data. About 10,000 hours of robot data is tiny next to the trillions of tokens behind language models. Human video, simulation and world models that generate training data are the main bets for closing that gap.
  • Evaluation. There's no ImageNet for robots, and real-world results rarely reproduce across labs.
  • Memory and long tasks. Most VLAs see only the last frame or two. Tasks that take minutes need memory and planning.
  • Touch. Almost every VLA is vision-only. Contact-rich work like insertion, cloth and tool use needs force and tactile sensing. That's close to my own work on in-hand manipulation with Acti-V-Link.
  • Speed. A 3B model at 50 Hz on an edge computer is still tight. Distillation and System 1 / System 2 splits are the current answers.
